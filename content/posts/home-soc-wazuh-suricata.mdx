"Set up a SOC" is a strange thing to attempt with a Raspberry Pi and a home network with maybe a dozen devices on it. There's no real incident volume to respond to. But the pieces (network detection, log aggregation, alerting) work the same whether you're watching a household or a datacenter, just at a scale where you can actually see everything that happens.

## The stack

- **Suricata**, running in inline mode on the router, doing signature-based network intrusion detection against a ruleset pulled from Emerging Threats
- **Wazuh**, aggregating Suricata's alerts plus host-level logs from a couple of Linux boxes, and applying its own rule correlation on top
- **A dashboard**, because staring at raw JSON logs gets old within about ten minutes

## What it actually catches

Mostly noise, at first. Default rulesets flag a lot of things that are technically-suspicious-but-actually-fine: a phone doing DNS-over-HTTPS lookups that look like tunneling to a signature written for something else, a smart TV's telemetry traffic tripping a rule meant for botnet check-ins. Tuning that out is most of the actual work of running detection tooling, not writing new rules, but disabling or scoping the ones producing false positives without accidentally disabling the one that would have caught something real.

The genuinely useful catch so far: a misconfigured container that started making outbound connections to a port associated with a known cryptomining pool, not because anything was actually compromised, but because I'd copy-pasted a docker-compose file from a tutorial without reading what one of the exposed ports was for. Suricata flagged the outbound traffic pattern within about a minute of the container starting. That's the whole pitch for network-layer detection: it catches things host-level tools miss, because it's watching what's actually going over the wire instead of trusting a process list.

## What a real SOC does that this doesn't

This setup has no on-call rotation, no playbooks, no case management, and one person deciding whether an alert matters. A real detection team is mostly about *process* at scale: triage queues, escalation paths, and enough historical baseline to know what "normal" looks like for thousands of hosts instead of a dozen. The tooling here is genuinely close to what's used professionally. The organizational layer around it is the part a home lab can't really simulate, and honestly the part I'm least equipped to speak to yet.
